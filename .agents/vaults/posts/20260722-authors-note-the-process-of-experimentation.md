BURNOUT is an experimental series - the process of making it is equally - if not more - important as the actual story itself. After all, it is an exercise in upskilling on various fields and an effort born out of a passion for tackling multi-disciplinary and cross-functional projects (continues).

But let’s get back to the process.

Follow the experiment.

The Story

Deconstructing a seemingly superficial story of travel and injecting a little bit of drama into it (103 days of backpacking across 3 countries) still kept the surface level at the center of the narrative.

It had no grit. No backbone.

That’s where the concept of burnout came in: the emotional layer beneath all this fancy scenery and drone shots. It marked the beginning of a conflict.

And there’s really no good story without a conflict.

But that wasn’t enough. Feeling restless after years of not taking a vacation? Big deal. Undoing your life and embracing solo travel? There’s no resonance for viewers and readers alike - hell, there’s no resonance for the creator, either.

So, the more analytical the deconstruction of the story became - and the more testing on social media affirmed these assumptions of “bleh”, the more organic it felt that there was a deeper layer, the core layer.

Freud’s civil war (cited) - the bullshit we tell ourselves.

Now that, will absolutely resonate. And it did in closed testing.

But in order for it to work, the narrative needs to completely embrace absurdity, maintain an anti-mainstream attitude across the entire story and point the middle finger to the superficial beauty of social media.

It’s gotta make you uncomfortable, because the conflict is internal.

The Characters

Composition tests for the project’s “In a Summary” videos, ft. George Kary. The globe is an important prop for the Ego character.

To offset the bluntness of the “travel documentary” effect, we needed to satisfy one more condition for effective storytelling.

We needed a hero for the conflict.

Or an anti-hero (ref).

This was easy - having Freud’s work laid out in front of us, deciding on the 3 main characters was instant: the Id, the Ego and the Superego. What we had to figure out was the binding thread between them.

Sure, they’re arguing.

But why?

We had to connect the story’s concept of burnout with that internal misalignment we all feel at various parts in our lives. How the id craves pleasure and comfort; how the superego stands judgemental; and how the ego rationalises.

Now that last part - the rationalisation - began to resonate with the conflict and the story’s veil of absurdity.

You can tell who’s the asshole easily.

But there’s still something wrong, as we employ various narrative devices at most, if not all, unexpected places, setting the stage for the conflict’s climax and the ensuing plot-twist resolution at the end.

A good story leaves an after-taste; one that resonates personally.

So, we had the journey - and we had the hero.

It was time to get back to the technical aspects, for without them, even a good story with good characters can suck.

The Mechanics

George Kary (left) and Thanos Doumas (right) discussing the challenges of a DIY setup, during a test lighting session.

First of all, simply to conclude the conjunction (and juxtaposition) of the story with the characters clashing, it just made good sense to put a simplifying label on the whole thing - and thus, the 3 parts were born:

Dissonance ~ set in Italy, backwards

Resonance ~ set in Greece, forwards

Convergence ~ set again in Greece, but backwards (the last chapter is whimsically called Origins of Delusion)

Then we had to figure out lighting, composition, photography and videography from scratch (video editing is relatively easy to learn, in comparison). It was important to capture a similar Christopher Nolan vibe across all POVs, including Author’s Notes and BTS (inspired by Tenet’s (cited) palette).

That’s new media and cinematic for you. The story itself feels like you’re in someone’s head, hence the intense colors, dead pans and center placed characters. The Ego is, well, a Netflix-styled interview, but, absurd.

Then came theming, sound design and audio mixing. Nothing fancy, but the goal was to make something that’d be worth going back to years from now.

And finally, formats and channels for publishing the series (and the book).

It’s all pretty straightforward.
